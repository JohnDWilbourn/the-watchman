Wise as a Serpent, Gentle as a Dove

Sin, Evil, and the Precision of Jesus — What the Institutional Church Has Collapsed and Why It Matters

August 2026  ·  John David Wilbourn  ·  Hamartiology  ·  Ethics  ·  Christology

The Command That Contains Two Different Things

When Jesus sent the twelve out in Matthew 10:16Loading…, he gave them an operating principle that the institutional church has spent two thousand years systematically dismantling: be wise as serpents and innocent as doves. The command contains two distinct instruments for two distinct categories of encounter. The dove governs the response to personal sin. The serpent governs the response to evil. They are not interchangeable. They are not to be collapsed into one another. And the collapse of the serpent into the dove — which is precisely what the institutional church has done — has produced catastrophic results for the people in its care.

The failure is doctrinal before it is pastoral. It begins with the refusal to maintain a precise distinction between sin and evil as different categories of conduct. Sin is a transgression — a crossing of a known boundary, an act incurring a penalty that can be paid. Evil is a different category entirely. Evil is an orientation of the soul, a disposition against God and against his design for human life. Evil thoughts are evil whether they are ever acted on or not. The thought is the thing. And the cross, which paid the penalty for personal sin in full, does not address evil on the same terms — because evil is not a debt to be satisfied by a qualified substitute. Evil is dealt with by judgment and removal.

These are not subtle distinctions. They are written into the structure of the Law, demonstrated in the ministry of Jesus with surgical precision, and confirmed by the architecture of eschatological judgment. The failure to maintain them is not a minor oversight. It is a load-bearing doctrinal error with real victims.

The Decalogue as the Codified Boundary

The Ten Commandments are not arbitrary restrictions on human freedom. They are the architectural codification of the boundary between sin and evil. They mark the lines at which ordinary human transgression — crossable and recoverable — gives way to conduct that carries an evil dimension: the systematic destruction of covenant, family, life, truth, and the community structures God designed for human flourishing.

This is why the encounter between Jesus and the wealthy man in Mark 10:17–22Loading… is more theologically significant than the institutional church recognizes. The man asked what he must do to inherit eternal life. Jesus responded by citing the commandments. The man answered that he had kept them from his youth. And Jesus, looking at him, loved him.

That detail — Jesus loved him — is not decorative. It is the Author's precise evaluation of a man who had maintained the Decalogue boundary throughout his life. He had not crossed into the evil dimension of the conduct those commandments guard against. Jesus honored that. He did not dismiss it, minimize it, or reframe it as self-righteousness. He saw it for exactly what it was and responded with love.

The evangelical tradition reads this story almost exclusively as a failure narrative — the man went away sad because he had great wealth, and the point is that wealth can be an idol. That reading is not wrong as far as it goes. But it misses what Jesus was doing in the first half of the exchange. The Prince of Grace — the one who came to pay the penalty for personal sin — took the keeping of the commandments with complete seriousness, because he authored them and he knows exactly what they are guarding. A man who has kept that boundary his entire life stands in a different position before the Author of the Law than a man who has not. Jesus saw that and named it with love before he named what was still lacking.

The Woman, the Men, and the Writing in the Sand

The account of the woman caught in adultery in John 8:1–11Loading… is among the most precisely constructed scenes in the Gospel narratives, and it is almost universally misread as a simple story of grace and forgiveness. It is not. It is a forensic document in which the Author of the Law administers it with perfect knowledge of every party's actual standing — and the distinctions he draws are exact.

The text does not establish that the woman was married. It establishes that she was caught in the act. This matters. The charge of adultery in the scene may rest entirely on the man's marital status — she may be the unmarried party in an arrangement initiated and sustained by a married man. And that man is conspicuously absent. The Law of Moses that the Pharisees invoke required both parties to be brought. Only she is present. The Pharisees are not interested in adjudicating adultery. They are using her as a legal instrument to construct a trap for Jesus. That is evil operating under the cover of legal righteousness — the precise profile of the Pharisaical system throughout the Gospels.

Jesus stooped and wrote on the ground. They pressed him. He stood and said: let the one without sin among you be the first to throw a stone. He stooped and wrote again. They left, beginning with the oldest.

They did not leave because they felt generically convicted of being sinners.
Every man in Jerusalem was a sinner.
They left in a specific order, beginning with the oldest.

The oldest had the longest record. Whatever Jesus wrote on the ground was specific enough that the senior men recognized it first and could not remain. Whether he was writing names, or a specific charge against a specific man, or the record of the arrangement that had produced this moment — he wrote what he knew. And he knew everything about every man in that crowd, because the one stooping in the dust was the Author of every life present.

When the accusers were gone, Jesus addressed the woman directly. Has no one condemned you? Neither do I condemn you. Go, and from now on sin no more. He did not pronounce forgiveness in the explicit sense he extends it elsewhere in the Gospels. He did not say your sins are forgiven. He sent her forward with a command. That is a precise judicial response calibrated to her actual position in the full complexity of what had just occurred. She was not condemned. She was also not treated as though nothing had happened. She was sent to the threshold she still stood at, with a command about which direction to walk.

The institutional church reads this as pure grace. Jesus was being gracious. But the precision is the point — and the precision requires the sin/evil distinction to see correctly. The evil in the room was not hers. It belonged to the men who selected her, arranged the situation, excluded the man, and brought her as a legal instrument. Jesus dealt with that evil by writing in the sand until the room emptied. He dealt with her conduct by sending her forward. These are two different responses to two different categories, administered simultaneously by the one who had complete knowledge of both.

Absalom: The Old Testament Case Study

The narrative of Absalom in 2 Samuel 15Loading… is the most fully developed case study of evil in the Old Testament, and it is almost never taught as such. It is treated as a story of a rebellious son and a grieving father. It is both of those things. But the conduct it documents is not the conduct of a man committing personal sins in weakness. It is the sustained, calculated operation of evil across multiple categories simultaneously.

Absalom spent four years positioning himself at the gate, stealing the hearts of the men of Israel, and systematically undermining David's judicial authority. That is not a moment of transgression. That is an orientation of the soul expressed through deliberate long-term strategy — evil by definition, invisible to any framework that can only see personal sin.

The violation of David's concubines on the rooftop was executed in full public view on Ahithophel's counsel, specifically to make reconciliation with David impossible. The women were instruments. The act was a calculated political claim of territorial dominance. Its surface resemblance to personal sexual sin conceals its actual character entirely. The evil dimension is not incidental. It is the entire point of the act.

David's response across the whole narrative is the pastoral tragedy of dove gentleness systematically misapplied to evil. He mourned when he should have acted. He restored when the situation demanded a different response. He wept at Absalom's death — my son, my son — for a man who had been driving him out of his own kingdom with deliberate calculation for years. David's love for his son was genuine. But it was categorically misapplied. He could not see the difference between a wayward son whose personal sins could be absorbed by a father's forgiveness and an agent of evil operating under the cover of that filial relationship.

Joab understood the distinction. That is why Joab killed Absalom against David's explicit orders, and why he rebuked David for mourning him afterward. Joab was not the more righteous man in the narrative. But he was the man operating with serpent wisdom where David was operating with dove gentleness alone. The serpent and the dove are both required. Without the serpent, the dove does not survive.

What the Cross Covered and What It Did Not

The atonement covered personal sin. Every personal sin of every human being — believer and unbeliever alike — was imputed to Christ at the cross and judged there. The penalty is paid in full. That is the basis on which God can extend the gospel offer to anyone. The legal obstacle has been removed.

Evil was not judged at the cross on the same terms, because evil is not a debt with a payable penalty. Evil is an orientation — a disposition of the soul against God and against his design — and it is dealt with by judgment and removal at the Great White Throne. The workers of iniquity answer there. Not before.

This means there are no deathbed conversions for the workers of iniquity in the sense the institutional church imagines. The words that could have interrupted the trajectory of a life given over to evil were available the whole time and refused the whole time. Proverbs 1:24–28Loading… makes this plain: wisdom called and they refused, she stretched out her hand and no one paid attention, therefore when their dread comes like a storm she will not answer. The deathbed is not a new opportunity. It is the terminus of a direction already chosen and confirmed by a life's work.

Romans 1:24–28Loading… describes the progression in its starkest terms: God gave them over. This is not a single moment. It is the divine response to a sustained trajectory — a life repeatedly choosing the orientation that defines evil. A person who has spent decades using the things of God as weapons and cover, who has committed sustained evil against the defenseless, who has deployed false witness as a tool of destruction — that person has been moving through that progression for years. The words at the end do not reverse a life's direction.

This also means the forgiveness command is category-specific. When Scripture commands the forgiveness of a sinning brother — and the language in Matthew 18:21–22Loading… and Luke 17:3–4Loading… is specifically about personal sin — that command rests on the fact that personal sin has a payable penalty and God himself has modeled absorbing that cost. The cross is the basis for forgiving personal sin. Evil committed against you is not in that category, because it isn't covered by the same mechanism. Requiring a believer to forgive evil on the same terms as personal sin is not only doctrinally incoherent. It tells the victims of evil that their response to it must mirror their response to ordinary transgression. It silences them with the vocabulary of grace. That is one of the most damaging things the institutional church does.

The Thief, Nathanael, and Kenotic Precision

The institutional church has constructed a sentimental Jesus who forgives everything indiscriminately and asks no hard questions. The actual Jesus operated with kenotic precision — voluntarily constrained to the Spirit's power rather than the independent exercise of his divine attributes, but not for a single moment naive, credulous, or operating without full knowledge of every person and situation he encountered.

The thief on the cross in Luke 23:39–43Loading… is the case the institutional church reaches for first. Jesus forgave a dying criminal in his final moments. Therefore grace is available to anyone at any time regardless of what they have done. The conclusion is not wrong. The application is almost always wrong. The thief on the cross had one moment of genuine faith in the physical presence of the Son of God, in circumstances that stripped away every pretense and every alternative. That faith was real. It was sufficient. It was honored immediately and completely. But it is not a template that covers every deathbed claim, and it does not mean that a life given over to evil can be resolved by words in a final hour.

Nathanael under the fig tree in John 1:47–51Loading… is the case the institutional church rarely preaches. Before Philip introduced them, Jesus saw Nathanael. Specifically. He knew him. Nathanael's response was immediate and total — Rabbi, you are the Son of God, you are the King of Israel — because he recognized that only the Author of his life could have that knowledge. The same Jesus who forgave the thief saw Nathanael under the fig tree before any introduction was made.

He knew Judas from the beginning. He told the disciples one of you will betray me and looked straight at him. He washed his feet. He extended every opportunity across three years of daily proximity. And he let it happen on the Father's timetable with full knowledge of exactly what Judas was and what he was doing. That is not a foolish simp. That is sovereign precision operating through perfect kenotic obedience.

Kenotic obedience is not credulity.
The serpent and the dove operated simultaneously in Jesus
in every encounter, without exception.

The serpent wisdom is the capacity to recognize evil for what it is, name it accurately, and respond to it without the naivety that conflates it with personal sin. The dove gentleness governs the response to personal sin — patience, forgiveness, restoration. Jesus deployed both throughout his ministry without confusion between them. He forgave sins. He called the Pharisees a brood of vipers to their face and refused to engage their traps as though they were sincere questions. He honored the wealthy man who kept the commandments. He wrote in the sand until the room emptied. He received the thief's faith and promised him paradise. He saw Nathanael before Nathanael saw him.

This is the Jesus the institutional church has dismantled by collapsing the serpent into the dove. The result is a Jesus who cannot protect anyone because he cannot name what threatens them.

◆

A Word to the Reader

If you have been told that faithfulness requires you to forgive evil on the same terms you forgive personal sin — that the same grace that covers transgression must also cover the sustained, calculated destruction of the defenseless — you have been given a doctrine that the cross does not support, that the ministry of Jesus does not model, and that Matthew 10:16 directly contradicts.

The forgiveness command is real. It governs the response to personal sin and it is not optional. But it is category-specific. It is grounded in the atonement's coverage of personal sin. It is modeled by a God who absorbed the penalty for transgression at enormous cost. It does not extend by the same mechanism to evil, because evil is not covered by the same mechanism.

The serpent and the dove are both given in the same command. Both are required. The dove without the serpent is not more Christlike — it is less, because it produces a believer who cannot see what Jesus saw with perfect clarity in every encounter. A believer who cannot name evil cannot protect themselves or anyone in their care from it.

The wealthy man kept the commandments and Jesus loved him. The woman caught in adultery was sent forward without condemnation and without simple forgiveness. Absalom was destroyed by a father's dove gentleness misapplied to evil. The thief on the cross was received in his one genuine moment of faith. Nathanael was seen under the fig tree before he arrived. Judas was known from the beginning.

Jesus operated with serpent wisdom and dove gentleness simultaneously, in perfect kenotic obedience, without confusion between the categories. That is the operating principle his people were given in Matthew 10:16. It is time to use both instruments he provided.
